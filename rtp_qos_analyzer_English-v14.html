Methodology

RTP/RTCP detection: every UDP packet whose source or destination port falls within the given range is inspected. A packet is classified as RTCP if its version is 2 and the second byte indicates a packet type of 192–223 (SR/RR/SDES/BYE/APP); otherwise, if it has at least 12 bytes and version 2, it's treated as RTP. This is the same heuristic Wireshark's dynamic dissectors use.

Grouping into calls: each RTP flow is identified by its tuple (source IP:port → destination IP:port, SSRC). RTCP reports (SR/RR) are matched to the flow whose SSRC matches the report's "source SSRC" field, not by port convention, so they still work even when RTCP doesn't use the usual port+1.

SIP signaling and SDP: the app looks for SIP messages across all UDP (and TCP) traffic in the trace (not just the port range given for RTP/RTCP, since signaling and media conventionally use different ports). It groups messages by Call-ID into dialogs, and from any SDP body (INVITE, 200 OK, etc.) extracts the media port (m=audio ...) and the a=rtpmap lines that map each payload type to its codec and the real clock rate negotiated for that specific call.

Clock rate: calculating jitter/skew from RTP timestamps requires knowing the codec's sampling frequency, determined with this priority: 1) if the SIP signaling's SDP explicitly negotiated that payload type for this flow's port, that frequency is used (the most reliable source, since it's what was actually agreed for the call). 2) if there's no SDP but the payload type is static (RFC 3551, e.g. PT 0=PCMU/8000, PT 8=PCMA/8000, PT 18=G.729/8000), the standard's fixed frequency is used. 3) for a dynamic type (96–127) with no SDP available, it's estimated automatically by comparing how the RTP timestamp advances against the real arrival interval, picking the common frequency (8000/16000/32000/44100/48000/90000 Hz) that best fits — this correctly detects cases like Opus, which RFC 7587 fixes at 48000 Hz even in narrowband audio mode. You can override the rate manually from the "Clock rate" selector if it doesn't match the real codec, and every metric for that flow recalculates instantly.

Jitter and Skew: calculated with the standard RFC 3550 formula from the difference between the real arrival interval and the interval expected from the RTP timestamps. Jitter is that difference smoothed (a moving average); skew is the same difference accumulated without smoothing, which is why it reveals sustained clock drift.

Packet loss: detected from gaps in the RTP sequence number (with 16-bit cycle reconstruction for long calls).

Jitter thresholds (RFC 3550 / common VoIP practice): < 20 ms excellent, 20–50 ms acceptable (the receive buffer usually compensates for it), > 50 ms poor. The "min" shown next to average jitter is the lowest jitter estimate (RFC 3550) observed across the whole flow, excluding the first packet (it has no computable jitter, since there's no previous one to compare against).

Clock drift — Skew (ms): cumulative skew displacement across the whole call (see the explanation under the skew chart). < 20 ms stable, 20–100 ms noticeable drift, > 100 ms significant drift — over long calls this can eventually cause glitches from the receive buffer overflowing or running dry.

Freq. Drift (ppm): the same drift expressed as the relative difference between the sender's and receiver's sampling clocks, in parts per million — ppm = (skew slope per packet ÷ packetization interval) × 10⁶. Rough thresholds: < 50 ppm within a typical quartz oscillator's tolerance, 50–150 ppm caution, > 150 ppm critical. This isn't a fixed ITU-T standard, it's an engineering rule of thumb — if the value looks extreme, cross-check it against the accumulated skew in ms (more direct, and equally reliable) before trusting the ppm figure.

"No sustained drift confirmed": this notice appears when driftDetected is false even though the ppm or max skew look high. It happens because ppm is calculated over the whole skew range, and a single isolated network hiccup (a gap of a few hundred ms followed by a quick catch-up) can inflate that figure even though the net displacement by the end of the call is small. In that case, check whether the skew returns close to 0 by the end (an isolated hiccup, normal on real networks) or keeps growing monotonically to the end (real clock drift) — the skew chart shows this visually.

Packet loss thresholds: < 0.5% excellent, 0.5–3% acceptable, > 3% poor (beyond this the human ear usually notices dropouts).

Bandwidth: calculated packet-by-packet as size/arrival-interval, with a 2 ms floor on the interval so that two near-simultaneous packets (common in real captures due to bursts or reordering) don't send the value to absurd figures. The "average bandwidth" shown is the median of those values, not the arithmetic mean, precisely so a handful of one-off spikes don't distort the number representing the whole call.

Capture artifacts: when a flow shows high jitter with inter-packet intervals clustering into two well-separated groups (instead of varying continuously), the app checks whether the sender's own RTP timestamp advances perfectly evenly. If so, the irregularity didn't come from the sender or from the network the call actually experienced — it was introduced by how packets were delivered/timestamped at the capture point (batching, network interrupt coalescing, virtualization...). In that case a notice appears under the jitter chart, because both the calculated jitter and bandwidth stop faithfully representing the call's real quality.

Estimated MOS: a simplified heuristic — 4.5 − min(avg_jitter/10, 1.5) − loss% × 0.2 — not the full ITU-T E-model (which requires real end-to-end latency), it's a rough guide.

RTCP summary: when the trace contains SR/RR reports, the loss and jitter the receiver itself reported are also shown — useful for cross-checking against what this app calculates from the captured RTP packets. RTT (round-trip time) isn't calculated in this version, since it requires complete SR/RR pairs in both directions.

Jitter spikes / cadence cuts: packets whose jitter or delta exceed 4× the flow's median are flagged, as a sign of micro-dropouts or reordering.
